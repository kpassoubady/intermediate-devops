Empirical Science

What to Say: Notice the progression: DevOps was not invented by cloud vendors selling tools. It started as an organic reaction to Agile. Agile had compressed software development cycles down to two-week sprints, but releases still sat in queues for months waiting for an operations deployment window. In 2009, John Allspaw and Paul Hammond stunned the industry at the Velocity conference by showing that Flickr deployed ten times a day because dev and ops shared tools and ownership. Patrick Debois coined the term in Belgium shortly after.

Ask the Room: How many of you remember the days—or still experience them—where code releases meant midnight bridge calls and cold pizza?

Teaching Focus: Explain that DevOps was born out of real practitioner frustration, not vendor marketing.

The problem DevOps addresses

The two groups have opposite goals, and work moves between them in large, slow batches. DevOps practices give both groups one shared goal, which is frequent and safe change.

What to Say: In *The Phoenix Project*, Gene Kim calls this the "core chronic conflict". Look at how traditional IT structured bonuses and promotions: Developers were evaluated on velocity—shipping new features as fast as possible. Operations was evaluated on four-nines availability and stability. Therefore, to Operations, every single software change handed over the wall was a direct threat to their uptime and sleep.

The Vicious Cycle: Because releases frequently caused outages, Ops responded by adding longer freeze windows, manual test sign-offs, and Change Advisory Boards (CABs). Dev responded by cramming even more features into each release. The batch size ballooned, blast radius expanded, and when failure inevitably happened, finding the bug across 200 changes took weeks. DevOps changes the game by giving both teams a unified goal: small, continuous, safe delivery.

Ask the Room: Who has worked under a strict change freeze where nobody could deploy for weeks?

Teaching Focus: Unpack the structural misaligned incentives behind the 'Wall of Confusion'.

The Three Ways

The Three Ways are the principles behind most DevOps practices (Kim et al., 2021).

What to Say: The DevOps Handbook organizes all technical delivery practices into these Three Ways: First, Flow—moving work smoothly from left to right, from developer keyboard to customer, in tiny increments without waiting in queues. Second, Feedback—shortening and amplifying feedback from right to left so defects trigger an immediate stop-the-line response rather than lingering unnoticed. Third, Continual Learning—allocating time to improve daily work, conduct blameless incident reviews, and build automated guardrails that make experimentation safe.

Course Alignment: Today's labs are strictly built around the First and Second Ways: establishing automated pipelines (Flow) and fast, reliable pull request checks (Feedback).

Teaching Focus: Frame the Three Ways as foundational delivery physics, not abstract slogans.

Value streams and lead time

Lead time for changes is the time from commit to running in production (Forsgren et al., 2018). In this example the change spends most of its lead time waiting.

What to Say: Look at the stark reality on this value stream: an engineer does 6.5 hours of real keyboard work, but the change takes over a week to reach production. Why? Because over 90% of the lifecycle is dead wait time—waiting 2 days for a code review, 3 days for access to a QA staging environment, 4 days for a weekly change approval meeting.

The Core Insight: Telling developers to write code faster or work late nights moves the needle by less than 5%. If you want to compress cycle time, you must eliminate the idle queue time between stages. That is precisely why we automate testing on pull requests and automate environment provisioning with Infrastructure as Code on Day 2.

Ask the Room: Think about your current team: where does a change spend the most calendar days just sitting in a queue?

Teaching Focus: Expose the massive difference between touch time (actual work) and lead time (calendar wait time).

Little's Law (Operations Research, 1961) Average Lead Time = Work in Progress (WIP) ÷ Throughput

Example: 20 items in progress ÷ 5 items finished per week = 4 weeks lead time.
Takeaway: To shorten lead time without working faster or cutting corners, start fewer things at once.

What to Say: Think about root-cause debugging: if you deploy a massive release containing 200 merged PRs and users report crashes, your engineers must search across 200 variables and millions of diff lines to locate the fault. But if you deploy single logical changes hourly, debugging takes seconds: you inspect the single commit deployed ten minutes ago.

Little's Law in Plain English: Look at the math from queueing theory: Lead Time equals Work in Progress divided by Throughput. If your team starts 20 user stories simultaneously and delivers 5 a week, your average turnaround is 4 weeks. To cut lead time in half without working overtime or burning out, you don't type faster—you cap your work in progress. You start fewer branches at once, merge daily, and keep PRs small. That is the mathematical foundation of trunk-based development.

Teaching Focus: Connect lean manufacturing batch size and Little's Law to software delivery risk.

What to Say: The DORA research evaluated over 30,000 engineers globally to answer: "How do we measure delivery capability objectively?" They settled on four core metrics. Two measure velocity: how often code deploys, and how quickly a commit reaches users. Two measure reliability: what percentage of releases cause an incident, and how fast you recover when something breaks.

Crucial Instructor Warning: Emphasize strongly: DORA metrics measure team and system performance, NEVER individual human productivity. The moment leadership attempts to rank developers by deployment frequency, engineers will game the system by splitting single commits into 50 trivial PRs. Goodhart's Law takes over, and the metric ceases to measure real health.

Version Control and Continuous Integration

Branching workflows, trunk-based delivery, and automated checks

Transition: We move from organizational principles to engineering mechanics. In this module, we examine how Git's cryptographic commit graph powers automated CI, why trunk-based delivery replaced GitFlow, and how pipelines act as objective quality filters.

What version control records

  • A commit is a snapshot of all files, plus the author, time, message and parent commit.
  • Git names each commit by a hash of its content and its parents. Changing anything changes the hash.
  • A branch is a name that points to one commit. A merge commit has two parents.

What to Say: Everyone here uses Git, but let's connect Git's internal data model directly to automated pipelines. A Git commit is NOT a file diff; it is a full tree snapshot pointing to its parent hashes. Because each commit hash is computed using its contents, author, and parent SHA, any retroactive alteration to history completely changes every downstream hash.

Pipeline Custody: This immutability provides an unbroken audit trail: when Harness reports that execution #42 passed commit e91b, you have mathematical proof of what ran, and nobody can alter that code without producing a different hash.

Lab 01 Connection: In Lab 01 coming up next, we will configure Git, push our starter flight-status application with a personal access token, and establish the repository that our pipelines will monitor.

Teaching Focus: Explain why Git's cryptographic immutability is the prerequisite for all CI/CD pipelines.

Two branching strategies

The labs use trunk-based development with short-lived branches and pull requests.

What to Say: GitFlow was designed in 2010 by Vincent Driessen for shrink-wrapped, packaged desktop software where teams had to maintain multiple release versions simultaneously (like v1.2 and v2.0 patches). But in continuous web and cloud delivery, GitFlow creates severe integration drag: branches like develop, release, and hotfix stay separated for weeks, delaying feedback.

Author Retraction: In 2020, Driessen added a prominent note to his original blog post: for software that is delivered continuously, GitFlow is an explicit anti-pattern, and teams should adopt simpler trunk-based workflows.

Ask the Room: How many active branches does your team typically manage right now, and how long does a branch stay open before merging?

Teaching Focus: Explain why GitFlow created massive delivery friction and why trunk-based delivery is the cloud standard.

Trunk-based development

  • Developers merge small changes into main at least once a day.
  • Unfinished features are hidden behind feature flags, so main can always be released.
  • High performers had three or fewer active branches, branches that lived less than a day, and no code freezes (Forsgren et al., 2018).

What to Say: Trunk-based development does not mean pushing broken code straight to main. It means feature branches are extremely short-lived—lasting hours or at most a single day. When engineers merge tiny, coherent changes into main every day, merge conflicts are practically eliminated.

The Common Objection: People often ask: "How do I build a large multi-week feature if I have to merge to main every day?" The answer is feature flags (or branch by abstraction). You merge the code into main continuously, but wrap the new execution path behind a toggle so it remains dark in production until fully verified. This decouples code deployment from feature release.

Teaching Focus: Dispel misconceptions about trunk-based development and explain how to ship large features safely.

2. Peer Review

Microsoft Research (Bacchelli & Bird, 2013): Reviews catch far fewer syntax defects than expected. Their primary real-world value is knowledge transfer and shared code ownership.
The Small PR Golden Rule: Review turnaround speed and critical scrutiny drop precipitously as PR size grows. Keep pull requests scoped to single logical changes readable in under 20 minutes.

What to Say: The pull request is where automation and human judgment divide responsibilities. Notice the division of labor: Machines must check what machines can objectively prove—compilation, unit tests, code style, security vulnerabilities, and test coverage. Never allow expensive senior engineering time to be wasted debating indentation or missing semicolons in PR comments.

The Real Value of Code Review: Bacchelli and Bird's Microsoft research revealed that peer reviews rarely catch functional logic bugs. Their true real-world value is knowledge sharing, architectural alignment, and shared code ownership.

The 20-Minute Golden Rule: Review quality collapses as PR size increases. A 20-line pull request gets 10 insightful comments; a 1,000-line pull request gets an exhausted "LGTM" and an immediate merge.

Teaching Focus: Establish clear boundaries between automated checks and human peer review.

Continuous integration practices

  • Keep everything needed to build the software in version control.
  • Automate the build, and make the build test itself.
  • Everyone pushes commits to the main branch every day.
  • Every push to the main branch triggers a build.
  • Fix a broken build immediately. Keep the build under about ten minutes.

Fowler's article lists more practices. These five are the ones a pipeline enforces (Fowler, 2024).

Press ↓ for supporting detail: What is Continuous Integration?

What to Say: Martin Fowler defines CI as a practice: integrating work into the shared mainline at least daily, verified by an automated build that runs in under ten minutes. Why ten minutes? Because if a build takes forty-five minutes, developers lose focus, grab coffee, or switch branches. By the time a failure reports, the author has moved on and other developers have already stacked code on top of the broken build.

The Acid Test: Ask yourself: if your laptop died right now and you received a blank machine, could you clone your repository and run the full test suite with one single command? If you need a 10-page wiki or have to copy secret configuration files from a senior colleague, your CI foundation has gaps.

Teaching Focus: Present CI as an engineering discipline and cultural contract, not merely software tooling.

Deep Dive / Core Concept

What is Continuous Integration?

The Practice

Social Contract

Developers merge working code into the shared main branch daily. Small, frequent merges eliminate painful "merge hell" and expose conflicts while they are still tiny.

The Tooling

Automated Gatekeeper

An automated server (like Harness) builds and tests every commit within ten minutes, giving immediate feedback on code health and preventing broken code from reaching others.

Key Insight

Having a build server is not CI. A team that runs automated builds but merges only once a month is doing automated builds, not Continuous Integration.

What to Say: For BAs, QEs, and mainframe engineers: remember this critical distinction. An automated build server like Harness or Jenkins is just software. Continuous Integration is a team social contract. If an organization spends millions on cloud CI infrastructure, but teams keep private feature branches open for four weeks before merging, they are not doing Continuous Integration—they are simply doing automated batch testing.

Teaching Focus: Reinforce the distinction between running an automated build and actually doing Continuous Integration.

Why integration delay is expensive

  • The longer two branches stay apart, the more both of them change, and the larger the conflicts.
  • A conflict found after three weeks involves code that its authors no longer remember well.
  • Integrating every day keeps each merge small and each problem easy to locate.

What to Say: Integration pain does NOT grow in a straight line with time. If two developers each modify five files today, the chance of overlap is negligible. But if their branches stay separated for three weeks, both have modified shared schemas, shared utility functions, and shared configs. The merge becomes an agonizing, multi-day archaeological excavation.

The Hidden Danger: Worse than textual conflicts are semantic conflicts: code that merges cleanly with no git errors, but crashes at runtime because assumptions changed. Continuous integration removes "integration hell" by making integration happen continuously throughout the day.

Ask the Room: Who has spent an entire afternoon untangling a merge conflict, only to break something else in the process?

Teaching Focus: Explain why merge conflict pain increases exponentially, not linearly, with branch age.

The deployment pipeline

A change moves to the next stage only if it passed the previous one (Humble and Farley, 2010).

What to Say: Jez Humble and Dave Farley formalized this pattern in *Continuous Delivery*. Notice how stages are sequenced: fast, cheap, and lightweight checks go on the left; slow, expensive, and broad checks go on the right. The Commit Stage takes 2–3 minutes to run unit tests and linters, giving developers immediate feedback. Only if that passes do we invest resources in slower integration tests and environment deployments.

Course Alignment: In Labs 02 and 03 today, we build the Commit Stage and Acceptance Stage for our flight-status service. On Day 3 in Lab 09, we complete the pipeline by wiring up automated Dev deployment, smoke tests, and production promotion.

Teaching Focus: Explain how stages are ordered to optimize feedback speed and resource cost.

Build once, promote the same artifact

  • Build the artifact once, in the pipeline. Deploy that same artifact to every environment.
  • Only the configuration changes between environments. Keep configuration in the environment (Wiggins, 2011).
  • If you rebuild for production, you deploy something that was never tested.

What to Say: Here is an immutable law of enterprise DevOps: Never rebuild code for different environments. You build the container image or application binary once in the pipeline, tag it with its cryptographic SHA256 digest, and promote that exact artifact through Dev, Staging, and Production.

Why Rebuilding Fails: If you rebuild from source for production, you might pull down a new patch version of a pip dependency released 10 minutes ago, or run against slightly different base image layers. What you tested in QA is no longer what runs in Production. The Twelve-Factor App mandates strict separation of config from code: the container artifact is immutable, and only environment variables change between clusters.

Teaching Focus: Explain why rebuilding source code for different environments is a major production hazard.

Pipelines and Harness

Automated build stages, container steps, and triggers

Transition: In this module, we dive into pipeline architecture and YAML syntax. We explore how Harness runs steps in ephemeral containers, why workspaces must be shared, and how local Harness Open Source maps directly to United's enterprise platform.

The parts of a pipeline

  • A trigger starts an execution: a push, a pull request, a tag, or a person selecting Run.
  • A pipeline has stages. A stage has steps that run in order. A failed step stops the stage.
  • The execution reports its status back to the commit and the pull request.

Press ↓ for supporting detail: Hierarchy of a pipeline

What to Say: Let's lock down our mental model for pipeline anatomy: The Pipeline is the blueprint code committed in Git. An Execution is one single run of that blueprint against a specific commit hash (for instance, Execution #12). Stages are major milestones (like a build stage or a deploy stage). Inside each stage, Steps execute sequentially in isolated containers. If any step fails with a non-zero exit code, the stage halts immediately.

The Git Handshake: Notice the status check arrow on the far right: this is the vital link between CI and Git. In Lab 03, we use this status check to enforce branch protection on main so untested code cannot merge.

Deep Dive / Mental Model

Hierarchy of a Pipeline

1. Pipeline & Execution

Blueprint & Run

Pipeline: The reusable definition in code.
Execution: One specific run for a commit (e.g. build #42).

2. Stage

The Major Phase

A logical group of steps sharing a purpose (e.g. ci stage to build and test; deploy stage to release).

3. Step

The Single Action

An individual command or container task inside a stage (e.g. clone, lint, test). If any step fails, the stage stops immediately.

Manufacturing Analogy

Think of an assembly line: The pipeline is the factory floor design, the execution is one car passing through, the stages are assembly stations (frame, engine, paint), and the steps are individual operations at each station.

The Stages are the major departments (Chassis, Engine, Paint). The Steps are individual automated robotic tasks at each station. Pipeline as code

kind: pipeline
spec:
  stages:
  - name: build
    type: ci
    spec:
      steps:
      - name: test
        type: run
        spec:
          container: python:3.12-slim
          script: pip install -r requirements-dev.txt && pytest
  • The pipeline is a YAML (YAML Ain't Markup Language) file in the repository: .harness/ci.yaml.
  • A change to the pipeline is reviewed in a pull request, like a change to the code.
  • The history shows who changed the pipeline, when, and why.

What to Say: Why is storing your pipeline in .harness/ci.yaml alongside your code such a critical engineering standard? Three major reasons: First, version synchronization—your pipeline syntax and your code dependencies change together in the exact same commit. You never suffer from a build script expecting Python 3.12 while the repository is still on 3.11. Second, peer review—changes to build flags or test arguments undergo PR review just like application code. Third, reproducible history—if you must patch an older release branch from six months ago, Git checks out the exact pipeline that verified it back then.

Teaching Focus: Explain why pipeline-as-code replaced web-UI job configurations in modern DevOps.

Containers as build environments

  • Each step names a container image, for example python:3.12-slim.
  • The image provides the tools. Nothing is installed on the machine that runs the pipeline.
  • Every execution starts from the same image, so a result can be repeated.
  • Pin the image tag. A floating tag such as latest can change between two runs.
  • Steps can use different images: Python for tests, Terraform for infrastructure, kubectl for deployments.

Press ↓ for supporting detail: Why run steps in containers?

What to Say: In legacy CI, build servers were fragile pets with multiple conflicting versions of Java, Python, and Node installed globally. If Team A upgraded Python to 3.12, Team B's legacy 3.9 job broke without warning. With containerized steps, the host machine only needs Docker. Each step brings its own disposable toolbox: python:3.12-slim for unit tests, hashicorp/terraform for infrastructure, or bitnami/kubectl for Kubernetes deployments. When the step exits, the container is destroyed—leaving no dirty temporary files or state behind.

Pinning Best Practice: Always pin specific image tags or digests in production. If you use latest, an upstream base-image rebuild over the weekend can break your build on Monday without a single line of code changing.

Teaching Focus: Explain how containerized steps eradicate the legacy 'snowflake' build server problem.

Deep Dive / Tool Isolation

Why Run Build Steps in Containers?

The Past

Snowflake Build Agents

Compilers and runtimes (Java 8, 11, 17; Python 2/3) installed globally on the build VM. Upgrades for Team A broke Team B's builds. "Passed on agent 1, failed on agent 2."

Modern CI

Disposable Toolboxes

Each step pulls an isolated, containerized image (e.g. python:3.12-slim). The build host only needs Docker. When the step exits, the container is destroyed without lingering side-effects.

Reproducibility Rule

Because the container image is identical on your laptop and in the pipeline, "it works on my machine" problems disappear: you can run the exact same container command locally.

What to Say: For non-developers and BAs: think of a containerized step like renting a specialty tool from Home Depot for 30 minutes. You don't buy, store, or maintain a commercial concrete mixer at home; you grab it, pour your driveway, and return it clean. That is what Harness does with python:3.12-slim: Docker runs the container for 20 seconds, executes pytest, and deletes it.

Teaching Focus: Explain containerized steps using the disposable tool rental analogy.

How Harness Open Source runs a step on your machine

Harness asks Docker to start one container per step. The steps reach Harness by the name harness on the devops network.

What to Say: Look at how Harness executes jobs on your lab machine: Harness itself runs in a Docker container, but notice the volume mount: /var/run/docker.sock is mounted into it from your host. This is known as Docker-out-of-Docker (DooD). When Harness needs to run your pipeline steps, it calls your host's Docker daemon and orders it to launch sibling containers on the shared devops Docker bridge network.

How Containers Communicate: Because all step containers share this devops network, the clone container can reach Harness at http://harness:3000 via Docker's embedded DNS. Notice also that every step mounts the exact same workspace volume.

Teaching Focus: Walk through the Docker-out-of-Docker execution model and container networking.

Steps share the workspace

  • Each step runs in a new container, with its own filesystem.
  • Only the workspace is shared. To pass files to a later step, write them into the workspace.

What to Say: Pay close attention here, because this trips up almost every engineer on their first pipeline: Containers are completely ephemeral; only the workspace volume survives across steps.

The Classic Failure Mode: If Step 1 runs pip install pytest, that package is written to /usr/local/lib/python3.12 inside Step 1's container. When Step 1 finishes, that container is completely deleted! When Step 2 launches in a brand-new container and runs pytest, what happens? pytest: command not found!

The Solution: You have two architectural options: either run your install and test commands sequentially inside the same step script, or install dependencies into a virtual environment created inside the mounted workspace directory. In Lab 02, we chain install and test within the same step.

Teaching Focus: Explain the #1 conceptual pitfall in containerized pipelines: ephemeral containers vs persistent workspace.

Background services

- name: db
  type: background
  spec:
    container: postgres:17-alpine
    envs: {POSTGRES_PASSWORD: test}
- name: integration
  type: run
  spec:
    container: python:3.12-slim
    envs:
      DATABASE_URL: postgresql://postgres:test@db:5432/postgres
    script: pytest -m integration
  • A background step starts a service and keeps it running while the later steps run.
  • Later steps reach the service by the step name as host name: db.
  • The service stops when the stage ends.

What to Say: Unit tests run in pure memory, but real production microservices interact with real databases. How do we test database queries in CI without sharing a dirty staging database that gets corrupted by concurrent runs? We use Background Services.

Lifecycle & Isolation: In Harness, setting type: background spins up a container (like postgres:17-alpine) that runs alongside your stage. Because both containers share the pipeline's internal Docker network, your integration test connects directly to hostname db on port 5432. When the stage concludes, Harness terminates the database container. Every test run starts with a completely pristine database with zero leftover data.

Lab 03 Warning: Postgres takes 3 to 5 seconds to boot and accept connections. If your test container tries to connect immediately, it crashes with "Connection Refused". In Lab 03, we write a quick polling loop to wait until port 5432 is healthy before running pytest.

Teaching Focus: Explain how background service containers provide clean, disposable dependencies for integration testing.

Conditions and expressions

- name: report
  type: run
  when: build.event == "pull_request"
  spec:
    container: alpine:3.20
    script: echo "Run ${{ build.number }}; Commit ${{ build.commit }}"
- name: notify
  type: run
  when: failure()
  spec:
    container: alpine:3.20
    script: echo "An earlier step failed"
  • ${{ … }} inserts a value when the pipeline runs: the build number, commit, branch or event.
  • when: decides at run time whether a step runs.
  • failure() runs a step only after an earlier step failed. always() runs it in every case.

What to Say: Expressions allow a single pipeline file to alter its behavior based on Git context. Harness dynamically injects variables such as ${{ build.number }}, ${{ build.commit }}, and ${{ build.event }}.

Real-World Conditional Patterns: Using when: conditions, you can define sophisticated rules: run fast unit tests on all PRs, publish Docker images only when merging to main or pushing release tags, and trigger incident alerts or automated rollbacks using when: failure(). On Day 3 in Lab 09, we use when: failure() to initiate an automated Kubernetes rollback whenever our health smoke tests fail.

Teaching Focus: Explain how runtime expressions and conditional logic make one pipeline file dynamically adaptive.

Deep Dive / Security Architecture

How the Harness Delegate Protects Networks

Control Plane (SaaS)

Harness Cloud

Hosts the web UI, pipeline graph definitions, audit trails, and execution status. It coordinates work without needing direct inbound access to private cloud resources.

Execution Plane (Private)

The Delegate

A lightweight agent running inside the organization's private VPC or Kubernetes cluster. It polls Harness over outbound HTTPS only, so no inbound firewall ports are opened.

Connectors & Credentials

Connectors configure how the Delegate reaches internal targets (Git, clusters, container registries, secret managers). Sensitive credentials stay securely inside your network perimeter.

What to Say: Why does enterprise InfoSec approve this model? Because opening inbound firewall holes to allow an external SaaS vendor to SSH or kubectl into your internal VPC creates catastrophic attack vectors. By having the Delegate initiate only outbound TLS connections (port 443) to poll for work, United's network remains strictly zero-ingress. Production database and cluster credentials never leave the corporate boundary.

The test pyramid

Write many small, fast tests and a few slow, broad ones (Cohn, 2009; Vocke, 2018). Higher layers cost more to write, run and maintain.

What to Say: Mike Cohn introduced the test pyramid in 2009 to combat the dreaded "ice-cream cone" or inverted pyramid. In an inverted pyramid, teams rely almost entirely on slow, brittle End-to-End browser tests that take hours to run, fail whenever a UI button moves, and are excruciating to debug.

Healthy Proportions: In a sustainable CI pipeline, you maintain a massive, broad foundation of unit tests that run in milliseconds, a focused middle tier of integration and API tests verifying database queries and network boundaries, and a thin top layer of end-to-end smoke tests for critical user journeys.

Ask the Room: Where does your team spend the most testing time right now—waiting on slow regression suites, or running fast unit tests?

Teaching Focus: Explain how Cohn's test pyramid protects pipelines from the fragile 'inverted pyramid' anti-pattern.

Test doubles

DoubleWhat it does
DummyFills a parameter that the test does not use.
StubReturns fixed answers to calls.
SpyA stub that also records how it was called.
MockFails the test if the expected calls did not happen.
FakeA working but simpler implementation, such as an in-memory database.

The flight-status API tests use a fake, an in-memory store, in place of Postgres (Meszaros, 2007).

What to Say: Engineers often casually call every test substitute a "mock", but Gerard Meszaros formalized five distinct doubles: Dummies fill unused parameters; Stubs return hardcoded data; Spies record call counts; Mocks assert specific method interactions; and Fakes provide lightweight, working in-memory implementations.

The Danger of Mock-Heavy Tests: Tests that heavily mock internal interfaces become brittle: they break whenever you refactor private helper methods, even when the external behavior is completely correct. In our flight-status API, we use a Fake in-memory repository: it adheres to the exact same interface as PostgreSQL, keeping API tests blistering fast while avoiding brittle mock assertions.

Flaky tests

Definition A test that passes and fails on the exact same commit without any code changes.
1. Timing & Sleep Fixed timeouts, slow CI runners, async race conditions
2. Shared State Polluted test DBs, leftover static state, shared caches
3. Test Order Tests pass when run alone, fail when run in sequence
4. External Calls Unmocked network APIs, external DNS, time-of-day

Google CI Scale (Micco, 2016)

While only ~1.5% of test runs report flaky failures, almost 16% of all test cases exhibit flakiness across their lifecycle—generating thousands of false alarms daily.

The "Cry-Wolf" Trap & Remediation

Flaky tests train developers to ignore failures and re-run pipelines until green. Golden Rule: Fix or quarantine (isolate from the blocking gate) immediately to preserve trust in CI.

What to Say: A CI pipeline is only as valuable as the trust developers place in red builds. The moment you tolerate flaky tests—tests that randomly pass and fail on the exact same commit SHA—developers stop investigating failures. They just smash "Re-run" until it passes, completely defeating the purpose of CI.

The Golden Remediation Rule: Never allow a known flaky test to sit in your blocking pull request gate. The moment a test exhibits flakiness, immediately quarantine it (move it to a non-blocking test group with an urgent engineering ticket) to protect team confidence in pipeline feedback.

Anti-Pattern Alert: Never use fixed sleep 5 commands to wait for databases or microservices in CI; on a busy multi-tenant runner, CPU throttling will eventually breach the sleep. Always use active TCP socket polling loops, as we do in Lab 03.

Teaching Focus: Expose how flaky tests destroy developer trust and establish strict remediation rules.

What code coverage tells you

def can_board(status):
    return status in ("SCHEDULED", "DELAYED")

def test_can_board():
    can_board("SCHEDULED")      # runs the line, checks nothing
  • Coverage measures which lines ran during the tests. This test reaches 100% coverage of can_board.
  • Coverage does not measure whether the tests check the results.
  • In five large Java programs, coverage had a low to moderate correlation with fault detection once the number of tests was held constant (Inozemtseva and Holmes, 2014).
  • A coverage threshold is still useful as a floor that blocks large untested changes.

What to Say: Look at the code on screen: test_can_board() executes the function, but contains zero assertion statements. Line coverage tools report 100% coverage, yet the test verifies absolutely nothing! If the function returned garbage, the test would still pass green.

The Academic Reality: Inozemtseva and Holmes' research proved that high code coverage has a very low correlation with real fault detection. So how should we use coverage? Use it as a floor, not a ceiling. A coverage threshold prevents developers from merging large new features with zero tests. In Lab 03, we enforce an 85% coverage threshold in our pipeline.

Teaching Focus: Expose the fundamental illusion of line coverage without assertion quality.

"Shift left" means moving checks toward the start of this line. A problem found in the editor costs one person a few seconds. The same problem found in production affects customers.

Quality gates on the main branch

  • A branch rule on main blocks direct pushes and requires a pull request.
  • The rule names the ci status check. Merge is possible only after the latest run of ci passed.

What to Say: A Quality Gate is an immutable automated policy rule. In Harness Open Source, we configure a Branch Protection Rule on main that enforces two non-negotiable constraints: First, it completely rejects direct git push origin main. Second, it mandates that all changes arrive via a Pull Request, and that the ci pipeline status check is GREEN before the merge button unlocks.

Remediation Flow: If tests or coverage fail, the merge button is disabled with an explicit rule violation message. The developer pushes a fix commit to their feature branch, Harness automatically reruns the pipeline, and only when the status turns green does merge become possible. In Lab 03, you will configure this exact rule and test it by attempting a forbidden direct push.

Instructor Delivery Track: Have everyone open labs/03-quality-gates.md. Preempt these four key milestones before they start:

1. Trailing Newline in VS Code: Ruff enforces trailing newlines. Part 0 provides a jq command to set "files.insertFinalNewline": true in settings.json so pasted files pass linting cleanly.

2. Direct Push Rejection: In Part 2, learners test their branch rule by trying to push directly to main. When Git rejects with "remote: branch main is protected", celebrate—it proves the gate works!

3. Database Polling Loop: Point out how the pipeline waits for PostgreSQL on port 5432 using a polling loop rather than a brittle sleep statement.

4. Intentional Bug & Lint Fixes: The new departures feature includes an intentional sorting bug and unformatted code. Students experience the full professional workflow: failing locally -> fixing code -> passing tests -> opening PR -> fixing linter in PR -> green merge.

Classroom Checkpoint: Ask participants to post 'Lab 03 Merged' in chat once their pull request successfully merges into main.

Pipeline evidence: Discussion

Explain to a new teammate why a green test run alone does not prove that a change is safe to merge.

  1. Think privately for 30 seconds. Name one check and the evidence it provides.
  2. In pairs, the explainer has 60 seconds. The listener asks: what could this check miss?
  3. Revise one sentence together. Include the check, its evidence and its limit.

Working alone? Write the explanation and challenge it yourself. Sharing aloud is optional.

Discussion Facilitation (2.5 minutes): Give the room 30 seconds of quiet reflection on the prompt: 'Why does a green test run alone not prove that a change is safe to merge?'

Debrief Insight: Call on one volunteer or read a chat response. Validate their reasoning: 'A passing test proves only what its assertions explicitly exercised. It cannot prove that edge cases weren't overlooked, that the code scales under production concurrency, or that the feature solves the right business problem. That is why automated gates and human peer review must operate together.'
